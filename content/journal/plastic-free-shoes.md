Every other garment you own has to tell you what it's made of. There's a law about it. Pull the tag on a shirt and you get percentages, fiber names, a country of origin.

Now look inside your shoe. A size. A brand. Maybe the word "leather," maybe "man-made materials," which means nothing at all. No percentages. No fiber list. Nothing about what tanned the leather, what glued the sole on, or what was sprayed on the outside to make water bead off it.

So the shoe that makes your feet sweat is usually made of plastic, and there is no label to tell you so.

### The sealed foot

The soles of your feet carry the densest concentration of sweat glands on your body, roughly 600 per square centimetre, matched only by your palms. That's a cooling system, and it's built to be open to the air.

A shoe closes it. Add a synthetic upper that doesn't breathe, a foam midsole that holds warmth, and a sock made of [polyester](/journal/polyester-is-plastic), and you've built a small humid room around the most actively sweating skin you own. Then you walk around in it all day.

That warm, damp, closed environment is the whole story. It's why athlete's foot exists. It's also the exact condition that pulls chemistry out of a shoe and onto your skin.

Sweat is the activator. It is the reason the same pair that felt fine in October starts causing trouble in July, and the reason a shoe's materials matter more than a shirt's despite touching thicker skin.

### What a shoe is actually made of

**Rubber accelerators.** These are the compounds used to vulcanise rubber, meaning to turn soft raw rubber into something springy and durable. They have names like thiurams and carbamates. In data from the North American Contact Dermatitis Group, rubber chemicals as a group were the single most common cause of shoe-related skin reactions, ahead of both adhesives and leather chemicals. Heat and sweat are what draw them out.

**Chrome-tanned leather.** Roughly nine out of ten leathers are tanned with chromium. The form used is trivalent chromium, which is fairly inert. The trouble is that it can convert to hexavalent chromium, a well-documented skin sensitiser, and that conversion is encouraged by heat, moisture, and age. The European Union capped hexavalent chromium in skin-contact leather at 3 milligrams per kilogram in 2015. The United States has no equivalent limit.

**PFAS.** The ["forever chemicals,"](/journal/is-your-activewear-toxic-pfas-in-leggings) used here as the invisible coating that makes water bead and roll off. Anything sold as waterproof, water-resistant, or stain-resistant is a candidate. Several states have moved to ban PFAS in apparel. Lawmakers have been slower to cover footwear, which leaves shoes one of the last places the coating is still routine.

**PVC and phthalates.** The material behind cheap synthetic uppers, jelly shoes, and a lot of children's footwear. Phthalates are the softeners that keep vinyl flexible, and they don't stay put.

**Solvents and glue.** Most shoes are assembled with adhesives rather than stitching. Synthetic "vegan leather" is often made using dimethylformamide, a solvent whose real cost lands mostly on factory workers. Residue is a smaller story than the manufacturing is, but it's part of what you're smelling in a new shoe.

**Antimicrobial insoles.** Anything marketed on odour control has been treated with something. Usually silver or zinc compounds.

> The shoe industry's answer to a sweaty synthetic foot has been to add a chemical that kills the smell, rather than a material that lets the foot breathe.

### That new shoe smell

Open a shoebox and the first thing that hits you is the smell. It gets described as new, which makes it sound like cleanliness. It is closer to the opposite.

What you are smelling is volatile organic compounds coming off the glue, the foam, and the solvents used to bond the shoe together. Volatile means they evaporate at room temperature, which is the only reason your nose can find them. The smell is the shoe releasing its manufacturing into the air around it, and it fades because those compounds are leaving.

That gives you a free test nobody sells you. A shoe that arrives smelling is telling you how much went into assembling it. Leave a new pair somewhere ventilated for a few days rather than in a closed wardrobe or a car, and treat a pair that still smells after weeks as a pair worth returning.

### What the traditions got right

Chinese medicine puts a single point on the sole and calls it a gushing spring. Ayurveda oils the feet before sleep. Kneipp built a nineteenth-century water cure around cold foot baths. Reflexology draws the organs across the arch. None of these traditions were talking to each other, and all of them arrived at the same instruction: keep the foot bare, warm, dry, and touched.

The explanations do not survive contact with anatomy. Nothing drains out through the sole, and the organ map has never been demonstrated.

The practice is another matter. In 1999, Kräuchi and colleagues at Basel published work in *Nature* showing that the opening of the blood vessels in the feet is the strongest physiological predictor of how fast a person falls asleep. Warm feet, around ten minutes. Cold feet, closer to twenty-five. A Bavarian priest with no physiology available to him had the protocol right by about a century.

That is the pattern for all of it. The traditions were reading a real signal off the feet. They wrote the wrong caption underneath it.

### What to look for instead

**Skip the waterproofing** unless you need it. It's the most reliable place PFAS shows up, and most people buy it for weather they never walk in.

**Read the lining, not just the upper.** A cotton canvas shoe with a synthetic lining has put the plastic on the side that touches you. Brands that use natural linings say so, because it costs them more.

**Vegetable-tanned over chrome-tanned.** Brands that go to the trouble of veg-tanning say so on the product page. Silence usually means chrome.

**Natural rubber outsoles**, and natural uppers: canvas, hemp, jute, wool, veg-tan leather. A plain canvas sneaker on a natural rubber sole is one of the cleanest shoes you can buy, which is a pleasant accident, because it's also one of the cheapest.

**Wide toe box, flat, flexible.** Your toes should be able to spread inside the shoe. This is a shape question rather than a materials one, and it's the one that changes how your feet age.

**Rotate your pairs.** A shoe needs a full day to dry out. Wearing the same pair daily is what turns the humidity problem chronic.

**Natural fiber socks.** [Wool](/guide/wool) or [cotton](/guide/cotton) rather than polyester. The cheapest fix on this list, and the one touching your skin most directly.

**Check the certification, not the adjective.** "Eco," "natural," and "conscious" mean nothing on a shoebox. [The marks that carry a standard behind them](/guide/certifications) are the ones worth reading, and footwear carries far fewer of them than clothing does.

### The short version

Feet sweat more than anything else on you, and a shoe is the only garment built to seal that in. The plastic is what does the sealing, and there is no label on a shoe to tell you where it is.

A warm, wet, enclosed space draws chemistry out of whatever it is made of and holds it against your skin for ten hours at a stretch. So the material question and the sweat question turn out to be one question.

The old advice was right all along, for reasons nobody involved could have known. Bare when you can. Breathable when you can't. Dry either way.
